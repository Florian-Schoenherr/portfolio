<ol class="principles">
  <li>
    <h3>Set the destination, not the route.</h3>
    <p>
      Outcomes belong to the manager; the path belongs to the team. The minute
      you start dictating the route, you've stopped buying judgement and
      started renting hands.
    </p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Trust is the default.</h3>
    <p>
      A specific reason can revoke it. Calendars, vague unease, and "the way
      we've always done it" cannot. Start everyone at green and let evidence
      move them, not anxiety.
    </p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Measure what shipped.</h3>
    <p>
      Velocity, story points, and hours-in-seat are proxies — and bad ones.
      Tie reviews to outcomes a customer or a balance sheet would recognise.
      Anything else is theatre.
    </p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Make the work obvious.</h3>
    <p>
      Decisions in writing, in the open, with the reasoning attached. If the
      project survives only because three people know things, you are one
      flu away from a postmortem.
    </p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Remove blockers faster than you set goals.</h3>
    <p>
      A good week is one where you mostly cleared the road. New goals layered
      onto unresolved blockers are just a heavier backpack.
    </p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>One owner per outcome.</h3>
    <p>
      Shared ownership is shared excuse. Two people accountable for the same
      thing means nobody is. Pick one, name them, and back them in public.
    </p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Feedback is a tool, not a reward.</h3>
    <p>
      Same week, specific, in private when corrective, in public when
      celebratory. Saved-up feedback is just resentment with a timestamp.
    </p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Hire people you would happily replace yourself with.</h3>
    <p>
      Then prepare to. The manager's job is to make the team less dependent
      on the manager, not more. If your absence is a crisis, you've been
      managing wrong.
    </p>
  </li>
</ol>
