People often ask me if I still trust the devices I use every day. The polite answer is "yes." But the honest answer is "no." And I do not think this makes me paranoid.

Think about it: My phone has a microphone, a camera, GPS, and many antennas. It runs on a system made by a huge company, and it has apps from other companies I do not know. My laptop is the same, just with a bigger keyboard. I cannot pretend that these devices are 100% safe. I read the news about data leaks. I know that security bugs are real, and I know that companies can track my internet browser. However, I do not want to panic all the time. If you worry too much, you are not actually safer—you just get stressed and might become careless.

Instead, I divide things into two sides. On the first side are the things I can control. My passwords are long and different for every account, and I store them in a password manager. Two-factor authentication is always turned on, and I install updates immediately. I never install software just because someone tells me to, and I always make backups to ensure my data is safe.

On the other side are the things I cannot change and simply accept. My phone shares metadata, my internet provider knows what websites I visit, and the operating system knows a lot about me. In 2026, this is the price we pay for using smartphones and laptops. I am okay with this price, but I want to be honest with myself about it. In the end, digital security is not about being afraid. It is about knowing what you can control—and what you cannot.

— Andreas
