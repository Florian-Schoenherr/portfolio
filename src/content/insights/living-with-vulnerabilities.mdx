People in our line of work get asked, with surprising frequency, whether
we still *trust* the devices we use every day. The polite answer is yes.
The honest answer is no — and I don't think no is the same as paranoid.

My phone has a microphone, a camera, a GPS, six radios, and an operating
system written by a company I have no relationship with, running apps
written by companies I have less of one. My laptop is a similar story
with a bigger keyboard. Pretending otherwise would require a kind of
selective amnesia I'm not capable of. I read the breach notifications.
I know the firmware bugs are real. I know my browser fingerprint is more
unique than my actual fingerprint.

So I don't ignore the vulnerabilities. I acknowledge them. But I refuse
to live as if every device is on fire, because that posture stops being
security and starts being theatre.

What I actually do is draw a line. On one side: the things I can
genuinely act on. Passwords are long, unique, and in a manager I trust.
Two-factor is on. Updates install the day they ship. I do not install
software because someone in a chat told me to. I do not connect my work
laptop to networks I would not connect my bank to. Backups exist, and
I have restored from them at least once on purpose, to know that I can.

On the other side: the things I have made peace with. My phone leaks
metadata. My ISP knows what I read. My OS vendor knows things about me
I do not know about me. These are the cost of using a smartphone in
2026, and I have decided that cost is worth it, on the condition that I
remain *honest with myself* about what I am paying.

The discipline isn't paranoia. It's knowing which battle is yours.

— Andreas
