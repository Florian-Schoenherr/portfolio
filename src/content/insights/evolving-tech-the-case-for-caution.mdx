I have nothing against new tools. I have a problem with the *idea* that
new always means better — that the cost of standing still is, by default,
higher than the cost of switching. In a small delivery organisation, that
idea is wrong about as often as it is right.

Every migration starts as a slide. It ends as six months of double-booked
brains. The team that was reliably shipping last quarter spends the next
two re-learning their own muscle memory in a slightly different shell. The
business gets a feature drought; the client gets the same product, with a
new bill of materials underneath. We're left arguing that *something*
must have improved, because the alternative is admitting we just paid for
movement.

The threats column is the one nobody puts on the slide. Drag is invisible
because it never shows up on a single quarter's report. It shows up two
years later, when half the team is fluent in *exactly the wrong version*
of three different platforms, and the senior engineer who knew the old
system has politely moved on.

The discipline I push for is boring: choose what to *not* adopt. Make the
default "we have this; it works; here is what we will need to see to
move." Treat every new platform like a hire — they have to make the team
demonstrably better, not just newer. If they cannot show that, the answer
is no, and "no" is a complete sentence in this business.
