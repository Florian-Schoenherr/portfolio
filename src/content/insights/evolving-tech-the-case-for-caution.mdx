I am not against new tools. What bothers me is the assumption that newer is
automatically better, and that not changing stack is always the riskier
choice. In a small delivery team, that assumption is about as often wrong
as it is right, or maybe more often wrong, if I am honest about the last few years.

That is not the same thing as refusing to touch dependencies. I have
refactored into different libraries and toolchain pieces quite a few times,
and those moves were rarely just for novelty. Usually it was efficiency:
less overhead, clearer maintenance paths, fewer sharp edges once the dust
settles - or updating because upstream support and security fixes dry up fast
these days and staying on deprecated bits becomes its own liability.

A migration usually looks tidy in a deck. In real life it tends to mean
months where the same people are trying to keep the old thing running while
they learn the new one. The team that was shipping steadily starts spending
time on setup, edge cases, and “how did we do this before?” The business
side still wants features; the client often ends up with the same product,
just rebuilt. Afterwards it is tempting to insist something *must* be
better, because admitting we mainly bought churn is awkward.

The threats side of the matrix is awkward to pitch upward, which is partly
why it gets skipped. Slowdown rarely lands in one quarterly report, but shows up later,
when knowledge is spread across half-updated stacks and the
person who understood the legacy piece has already left.

The approach I try to argue for is not exciting: decide what we will *not*
chase by default. Start from “we already have something that works,” and
list what evidence would justify a move. Compare a serious platform switch
to hiring someone - it should make the group clearly more effective, not
just newer on paper. If that case cannot be made, declining is reasonable,
and a straight “no” is still a legitimate answer.
